Industry · Startup

Testing a startup's core assumptions about older adults and caregivers

An early-stage startup was building an AI virtual assistant to help older adults with day-to-day tasks. My research with older adults and caregivers helped the team sharpen who they were building for, and why.

Role
UX Research Intern
Organization
Early-stage AI startup
Timeline
Summer 2026
Methods
In-depth interviews with older adults and caregivers

The challenge

The startup had a rough prototype of a virtual-assistant chatbot and was moving toward an MVP. The product rested on one core assumption: caregivers are the customer. Older adults were framed as a burden on their caregivers, and caregivers would pay for a product that eased it.

Investors were interested but wanted more proof of concept. The team brought me in to work backwards and gather evidence for that assumption.

My approach

  • Talked to both sides of the relationship. I interviewed caregivers and older adults rather than only the presumed buyer, so the research could test the assumption instead of just confirming it.
  • Chose methods that suit this population. I advised against surveys and short interviews, which tend to work poorly with older adults, and argued for longer, more relational approaches.
  • Treated participants as a vulnerable population. I reminded the team throughout that older adults need extra care in recruitment, consent, and how sessions are run.

What I found

  • Older adults want independence. They didn't see themselves as a problem to be managed, and they care about their autonomy as much as anyone.
  • Caregivers see support as love, not a burden. The "burden" framing at the heart of the pitch didn't match how caregivers described helping their family members.
  • Switching costs are high, especially for older adults:
    • Many don't want to text with a chatbot.
    • They want to escalate quickly to a real person.
    • They want dynamic, back-and-forth conversation.
    • Asking others for help is often a valued social connection, not friction that needs removing.

Impact

The findings challenged the team's early assumptions about who the customer is and what problem the product solves. By the end of my internship, the team had begun reframing the product's direction and its investor pitch around independence rather than burden.

What I learned

This internship taught me how to push back on methods and on founding assumptions respectfully, as a partner to the team rather than a blocker, while keeping user wellbeing and needs at the center. When research goes against what a team hopes to hear, how you deliver it matters as much as the findings.

Academic · Published research

The cruel optimism of staying: why people keep using dating apps that wear them down

Most users describe dating apps as draining, yet few actually leave. I studied how and why people stay, and found that hope and everyday coping keep users coming back.

Role
Lead researcher & first author
Organization
Northwestern University, with Supraja Sudarsan, Harper Barnowski & Calvin A. Liang
Timeline
Interviews Jun–Aug 2025; presented at DIS Jun 2026
Methods
15 semi-structured interviews; reflexive thematic analysis

The challenge

Research and popular media increasingly link dating apps to burnout, anxiety, and poorer well-being, yet user retention stays steady. That's a contradiction worth understanding: if dating apps feel so depleting, why do people keep using them? We asked how users' feelings shape their decisions to stay, and how they manage those feelings over time.

My approach

  • Interviewed 15 active dating app users aged 18–44, each for 55–75 minutes, and recruited until no new themes emerged.
  • Let participants name their own feelings and strategies, with prompts like "How do you generally feel after using dating apps?" and "What made you come back?"
  • Analyzed the interviews with reflexive thematic analysis, with three researchers coding independently in Dedoose. We used a feelings wheel to give the team a shared language for emotions without restricting what counted as one.

What I found

  • Staying runs on a cycle of emotional ups and downs. Recurring frustration, anxiety, and fatigue push people toward leaving, but brief moments of validation and, above all, hope pull them back in.
  • People build their own ways to keep going: emotionally detaching from bad outcomes, limiting how much they use the apps (for example by pausing their profile), and leaning on friends for support.
  • Those coping strategies make the apps bearable, which keeps people engaged even when the experience stays hard.

Impact

The paper was published and presented at DIS 2026 in Singapore. It gives platforms concrete design directions, including:

  • focusing users on fewer, more promising connections;
  • emotional check-in tools so users can notice patterns in how the apps make them feel;
  • better support for intentional breaks.

It also argues that design alone can't fix the deeper social and structural reasons people stay.

What I learned

Behavior that looks irrational from the outside, like staying on an app that makes you unhappy, makes sense once you understand people's feelings and the strategies they've built. It also taught me to be honest about what design can and can't solve.

Academic · Published research

Designing for authenticity: what BeReal gets wrong about how people are "real" online

BeReal tries to force authenticity through tight constraints: one random daily prompt, two minutes to post, no editing. I studied how users actually practice authenticity, and where the design works against them.

Best Paper Honorable Mention, CSCW 2025
Role
Lead analyst & first author
Organization
Northwestern University (Social Media Lab & CollabLab)
Timeline
Published Nov 2025
Methods
Secondary analysis of 25 interviews; reflexive thematic analysis

The challenge

Research suggests that sharing in ways that feel authentic can benefit well-being, so platforms are trying to design for it. BeReal takes the most rigid approach: it prompts everyone at a random moment and discourages late, retaken, or edited posts. But does a single, product-defined version of "authentic" match how people experience it?

My approach

  • Treated BeReal as a bounded case study, because its design makes authenticity an explicit product goal.
  • Reanalyzed 25 existing interviews with active BeReal users aged 18–24 (BeReal's largest demographic). My research group had collected them for a larger project on self-presentation, and I took a new angle: the tensions that shape how people practice authenticity.
  • Analyzed the interviews with reflexive thematic analysis, with two researchers coding independently in Dedoose before building shared themes.

What I found

  • Users experience two facets of authenticity: posting impromptu, in the moment, and representing themselves honestly. The two often conflict, and BeReal's design only supports the first.
  • Authenticity competes with other values. Users wanted privacy (the prompt might arrive at work or in class), autonomy over what they share, and the ability to reminisce about meaningful moments.
  • Users work around the constraints: posting late, changing the scene, or curating anyway, even though they know it goes against the app's intent.

Impact

The paper won a Best Paper Honorable Mention at CSCW 2025. It proposes an authenticity design space (how much control users have over when content is captured, what is shared, and how it's modified) and gives concrete design recommendations. For example, BeReal could send several prompts a day and let users choose one, keeping spontaneity while restoring some choice.

What I learned

Constraining users isn't the same as supporting them. Designing for a value like authenticity means understanding the contexts people live in (school, work, relationships) and giving them flexibility with some structure, rather than one fixed definition.

Academic · MA Thesis

Why people overshare on Hinge: privacy and self-disclosure on dating profiles

Hinge users know the privacy risks of their profiles, yet they still share a lot. I studied how the app's design and users' social norms push them to disclose more.

Role
Sole researcher (MA thesis)
Organization
Columbia University, Human Rights Studies; advised by Dr. Steven Bellovin
Timeline
2022–2023
Methods
App walkthrough; survey (n=75); 10 semi-structured interviews

The challenge

To use a dating app, people have to share personal details with a large, unknown audience. The usual assumption is that users are in full control of what they share, so people who share a lot must not care about privacy. I wanted to test that assumption by asking: how do Hinge's design and its users' social norms shape what people disclose on their profiles?

My approach

  • App walkthrough. I analyzed Hinge's marketing, privacy policy, and onboarding flows screen by screen to see how the product itself encourages disclosure.
  • Survey of 75 Hinge users (45 multiple-choice and open-ended questions), including a scenario question about how they judge a "sparse" profile.
  • 10 follow-up semi-structured interviews with survey respondents, analyzed together with the open-ended survey answers to identify the social norms around what to share.

What I found

  • The design nudges people to share more. The profile settings mix fields that are shown by default with fields that are hidden by default, so users have to pay close attention to stay in control of what appears on their profile.
  • Users know the risks. All 75 respondents named at least one privacy risk, most often unintended people seeing their profile, followed by stalking and harassment.
  • Users share anyway, because of social norms. "Sparse" profiles were judged as "low effort," so users felt pressure to share enough, while carefully choosing what to leave out to avoid being judged. On average, respondents shared 9 of the 20 optional profile details.

Impact

The thesis showed that heavy sharing isn't simply a choice people make freely: interface design and social pressure both push users to share more than they otherwise might. That points to design changes, such as clearer and consistent privacy defaults, that would help people make deliberate choices about what they share.

What I learned

Combining methods paid off. The walkthrough showed what the product encourages, while the surveys and interviews showed why people go along with it. Studying both the design and its users together gave a fuller picture than either could alone.

Research Fellowship

Screenshot, share, and save: why people post dating profiles on social media

People screenshot dating app profiles, their own and other people's, and post them to huge online communities. I studied why, and found privacy trade-offs that are more complicated than "sharing is bad."

Role
Research Fellow
Organization
Columbia Privacy Lab
Timeline
Published Sep 2023
Methods
Digital ethnography across 3 online communities; open coding

The challenge

Dating apps require people to share identifying details (name, age, location, face) with a large audience of strangers, and nothing stops other users from screenshotting a profile and posting it elsewhere. I kept seeing everyday users do exactly that, often with conflicting views and double standards. My research question: why are dating app profiles posted to Reddit, TikTok, and Facebook?

My approach

  • Picked three communities with different norms so I could compare the same behavior across platforms: the Facebook group "Are We Dating the Same Guy NYC," the r/Tinder subreddit, and the #Hinge hashtag on TikTok.
  • Ran a digital ethnography, taking field notes on the language, patterns, and etiquette of posts in each community.
  • Used open coding to build a set of post types and motivations directly from the data, rather than starting from fixed categories.

What I found

  • On Facebook, sharing works as a safety network. Most posts asked for "tea" or "red flags" on a man or warned others about him. Posters stayed anonymous while the men's faces and details were on full display, which is a privacy double standard, but one that the safety purpose depends on.
  • On Reddit, people gave up their own privacy to get help. Men mostly posted their own Tinder profiles asking for feedback, often sounding burned out and hopeless, while community rules had them blur other people's details.
  • On TikTok, sharing was mostly entertainment: viral trends that roast other people's profiles and voice prompts without their consent, alongside women sharing their own profiles to give advice.
  • Across all three, privacy was traded against other needs, such as safety, support, or entertainment. Looking only at privacy oversimplifies the problem: shutting these spaces down would also remove real benefits, like the safety network.

Impact

I published the findings in a Medium article, and the work was later cited in Wired. The research also raises design questions for dating platforms, such as how to make users safer without pushing the job of vetting matches onto them, and without causing new harm by shutting down communities people rely on.

What I learned

The same behavior can mean very different things on different platforms, and a single lens like privacy can miss what people are actually getting out of it. Comparing communities side by side showed me how much a platform's design and rules shape what people do.
